# Worked example — FDE 2.08: the demo that cut its favourite feature, annotated

Referenced by the FDE 2.08 learning session. The customer is
fictional; the pattern is a composite written for this course.
Annotations in brackets name the move being made. This example
uses a different customer from the exercise, on purpose: copy
the moves, not the words.

> **PROTOTYPE PLAN (excerpt) — Larkspur Freight
> quote-desk prototype, two-day sprint**
>
> **The question, written before the clock.** The desk
> lead's question is one sentence: would a dispatcher
> trust a draft quote assembled from the rate cards
> enough to edit it, instead of starting from a blank
> page? Everything in the sprint is priced against
> answering that, and nothing else is promised.
>
> *[A prototype without a written question becomes a
> small, rushed product — judged as a product, and
> found wanting as one.]*
>
> **The favourite, cut first.** The room's favourite
> request was live carrier-rate lookup — the highest
> value score on the list, and blocked on a carrier
> credential the vendor had not issued. It was deferred
> in the first ten minutes, in the plan, with the
> credential named as the thing that would reopen it.
> The sprint's credibility with the desk lead dates
> from that cut: the plan visibly obeyed its own rules
> against its own wishes.
>
> *[Deferring the blocked favourite early is the
> cheapest trust a prototype sprint ever buys.]*
>
> **Faked, out loud.** The margin-approval step was
> safety-adjacent — a wrong approval moves real money —
> so it was faked with a canned approval screen, and
> the presenter said, on the record, that the screen
> proved placement and nothing about approval logic.
> A dispatcher in the front row nodded; the label had
> made the demo more believable, not less.
>
> *[A labelled fake invites the audience to judge the
> real question. An unlabelled one asks them to judge
> your honesty later, at a worse moment.]*
>
> **What the demo decided.** Dispatchers edited the
> drafts; two of five asked for the blank page back on
> complex quotes, which became the production scope's
> first boundary. The prototype code was deleted the
> following week, by the plan's own declaration, and
> nobody mourned it — the harden list, eleven items,
> was the sprint's surviving artefact, and production
> was estimated from it, not from the demo's polish.
>
> *[Throwaway code that is mourned was never throwaway;
> it was production code acquired without production
> discipline.]*

**The lesson:** deployment speed is selection speed —
the rules decide what is deferred, faked, built, and cut
before the clock starts, so the 48 hours are spent
answering one question instead of negotiating twelve.
